Contract intelligence: documents to decisions

The rebate terms are three paragraphs buried in a sixty page contract, and there are 35,000 contracts. Reading all of them into a model costs a fortune and still misses things, so the job is to find the two paragraphs that matter and price the rebate off those.

Below is one contract going all the way through: it arrives, gets read, lands in the lake, is chunked into a vector index, and an agent pulls back only the clauses it needs. The rebate owed falls out at the end. A scaled-down version of a pipeline I built in production.

Capacity: committing before you know demand

Contract volume has to be committed for the quarter before anyone knows what the quarter holds. Commit too little and you buy the rest on the spot market at whatever it costs that week. Commit too much and you pay a shortfall fee on trucks you never used. The fleet is finite, so every load you promise one lane is a load you cannot promise another.

This is a two stage program with recourse, solved on 400 demand scenarios drawn from the same predictive the pricing panel builds. One price on capacity, the dual, applies to every lane at once, and the plan is the point where it meets the fleet exactly. The last beat is the part that matters: what this plan is worth against planning on mean demand, and how much of the remaining gap no plan could ever close.

Dispatch: pickup and delivery routing

A dozen orders, a handful of trucks, and nothing can be delivered before it has been picked up. Finding a set of routes that works is easy. Finding the cheapest one, and being able to show it is the cheapest, is the job.

Candidate routes are generated and priced, a set-partitioning pass picks the cheapest cover of every order, and the fleet drives it. Switch to Data for the instance as an operator would receive it, or Assignment to watch drivers get matched to chains of orders.

Pricing: quoting a lane nobody has run

A lane with five loads should not be quoted with the same confidence as a lane with a hundred, but it still needs a price today. The question is how much of the network's rate a thin lane should borrow, and how much of its own history it has earned the right to trust.

A three level Bayesian hierarchy answers that directly: the shrinkage weight below is how much of the quote came from the lane itself. Click any lane on the board, or use the arrow keys, to price it and watch the weight move.

About

I'm a data scientist. I like problems where someone has to act on the answer, which narrows things down a lot.

I studied math at New Mexico State in Las Cruces, a bachelor's in 2016 and a master's in 2018. My first job was business analyst at Chain Link Services, on supply chain and warehousing. Then three years at Knight-Swift in Phoenix, on freight. I've led data science at Stryker since August 2024, remote, on the medical device side. Day to day it's mostly Python and SQL.

The freight years are where I learned to build things people actually run. A driver retention model I rebuilt gained about 20% in F1 and fed into a 12% drop in turnover. A Bayesian study of lane-keeping sensor data, Poisson-Gamma in PyMC, put a real number on a safety question and backed a multi-million dollar fleet hardware purchase. A Gurobi scheduling model took one terminal from 20% planned drivers to 85%. That one I still like. Frequency-severity work sized a $60M insurance purchase.

Stryker is more commercial. Causal inference on rebate ROI, long-horizon revenue forecasting in XGBoost, a Databricks vector search pipeline over 35,000-odd contracts, a pricing benchmark engine. I mentor a senior data scientist and a few full-stack developers. Most weeks I'm somewhere between finance and pricing, with operations on the same call. Outside work: espresso, too much science fiction and fantasy, three cats, and walking around the city with my wife.
